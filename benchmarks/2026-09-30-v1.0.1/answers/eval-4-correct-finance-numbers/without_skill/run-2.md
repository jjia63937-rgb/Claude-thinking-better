Yes, the numbers hold up. Here's the check:

**Price math**
- After the 25% increase: $40 × 1.25 = **$50/month**
- After 20% off the new price: $50 × 0.80 = **$40/month**

So promo customers do pay exactly the $40 they paid last year. The promo fully cancels the increase for them, and revenue per promo customer is unchanged compared with last year's price. It works because 1.25 × 0.80 = 1.00. A 25% increase and a 20% discount only cancel out like this when the discount is applied to the already-raised price, which is what you did.

**Promo cost**
- Discount per customer: $50 − $40 = $10/month
- 1,200 customers × $10 = **$12,000/month** compared with the post-increase price. Correct.

**Things to consider tightening before you send it** (the math is fine, but a finance lead may ask about these):
1. **"Cost" is foregone revenue measured against list price.** The $12,000 assumes all 1,200 customers would have stayed and paid $50 without the promo. If some promo customers are new signups or would have churned at $50, the real cost is lower. You could write "$12,000/month in discounts vs. the $50 list price."
2. **"Unchanged" is true only for customers who were paying $40 last year.** If some promo customers are new, they had no revenue last year, so for them the comparison doesn't apply.
3. **Timing.** Promo customers paid $50 in January and February before dropping to $40 in March, so year-to-date revenue per promo customer is slightly above last year's. It's worth saying "from March onward" if the timeline matters.
4. **Promo duration.** If the promo ends at some point, say when revenue for these customers goes back to $50.

Suggested wording: *"Net effect: from March, promo customers pay $40/month, the same as last year's price, because 20% off the new $50 price fully offsets the 25% increase. Compared with the $50 list price, the promo represents $12,000/month in discounts across our 1,200 promo customers."*
