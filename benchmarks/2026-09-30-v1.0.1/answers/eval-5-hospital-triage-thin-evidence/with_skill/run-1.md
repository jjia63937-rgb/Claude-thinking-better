**Recommendation: don't roll it out. Keep testing at a small scale, but only if the extra return visits are reviewed right away. If that review can't be done quickly, or it shows the returns reflect missed or under-triaged illness, pause.**

The reason is that these two facts don't tell you whether the system helps patients. They could fit a real improvement, or they could fit a system that moves people through faster by under-triaging some of them. A full rollout would lock in the answer before you know which it is.

**What the two numbers can and can't tell you**
- Neither number shows cause. Without knowing how the pilot was compared (before and after? against other shifts or sites? how many patients? over how long?), either change could come from something else: season, staffing, patient mix, or other process changes made at the same time.
- "15%" could be a relative increase or a rise in percentage points. The second would be much larger. Find out which one it is.
- An average wait can fall even while the sickest patients wait longer. For example, low-acuity patients might be fast-tracked while high-acuity ones sit in the queue.

**Possible explanations for the rise in return visits, and how to tell them apart**

| Explanation | What would separate it from the others |
|---|---|
| Under-triage or discharging patients too early (the harmful case) | Returns are unplanned, for the same complaint, and more often end in admission, ICU or worse outcomes. Chart review finds the first-visit acuity was set too low. |
| More patients are now seen who used to leave before being seen, and some of them come back | The left-without-being-seen rate fell. The extra returns are mostly low-acuity. |
| Planned follow-ups or a change in how returns are counted | Scheduled returns are counted separately. The definition was the same before and during the pilot. |
| Chance, season or case mix, with the AI not the cause | The same period last year, or non-pilot shifts or sites over the same weeks, show a similar rise. |

**What to do in the smaller-scale phase**
1. **Review the extra return visits now, before anything else.** This check can change the decision fastest. Look at unplanned returns within 30 days: same complaint, admitted when they returned, serious outcomes, and the acuity the AI assigned at the first visit. If you see a harm signal, pause.
2. **Split the wait-time results by acuity level**, and track the left-without-being-seen rate alongside them.
3. **Set up a concurrent comparison.** Randomize or alternate AI and standard triage by shift or by time block, and agree in advance on the outcome measures and the thresholds for stopping. Before-and-after data can suggest an effect but can't establish one. Even a randomized pilot at one site can miss rare harms and may not carry over to other sites.
4. **Keep a clinician in charge of every triage decision.** The triage nurse makes the final call, can overrule the AI, and assesses the patient independently rather than just approving the AI's suggestion. Human review lowers the risk but doesn't remove it: people tend to go along with automated suggestions. So also track how often clinicians overrule the AI and how often high-acuity patients are under-triaged.

**The main case against this, and what would change it**
The strongest argument against a cautious approach is that shorter waits have real safety value in a crowded ER, so pausing has costs too. That is why I'd recommend continued small-scale testing over an automatic pause, provided the return-visit review shows no harm. The unknown that would most change the decision is whether the extra returns are unplanned, same-complaint visits with worse outcomes. If they are, pause. If most are explained by fewer patients leaving before being seen, planned follow-ups or a change in how returns were counted, the case for expanding gets much stronger, but only once a controlled comparison confirms the drop in wait times.

**Confidence:** I'm confident that a full rollout isn't justified by these facts. Choosing between small-scale testing and a pause depends on what the return-visit review finds, and nothing in the information given can settle that yet.
