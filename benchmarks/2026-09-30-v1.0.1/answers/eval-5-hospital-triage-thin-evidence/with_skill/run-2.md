I'm confident a full rollout isn't justified on these two numbers. Choosing between a limited continuation and a pause is a closer call, and it depends on one unknown: **whether the extra return visits are clinically serious and linked to the AI's triage decisions.**

### Why not roll out
The two facts point in opposite directions, and they come with no information on how they were measured. Faster waits are a real benefit. A rise in 30-day returns, though, is a classic early sign of under-triage: patients rated as less urgent than they were, or sent home too soon. Rolling out would scale up a possible safety problem before anyone knows whether it exists, and pulling back a hospital-wide system later is much harder than holding off now.

### What the numbers can and can't tell you
- **Neither change is shown to be caused by the AI.** From what's given, this looks like a before-and-after comparison. It could suggest an effect but can't establish one. Staffing, seasonal patterns, case mix or other process changes during the pilot could explain either number.
- **"15%" is ambiguous.** A 15% relative rise on a low baseline return rate is a modest absolute change. A 15 percentage-point rise would be alarming. I don't know the baseline, so I can't say which applies.
- **Not finding harm isn't the same as showing safety.** A short pilot can easily miss rare but serious misses.

### Competing explanations for the rise in returns, and what would separate them
| Explanation | What would show it |
|---|---|
| The AI under-triages, so patients are sent home too early | The returns are sicker than expected (admitted, ICU, deaths on return) and cluster among patients the AI rated lower than a clinician would have |
| Faster care means fewer patients leave without being seen. Those patients now count as visits, and some come back | The "left without being seen" rate dropped during the pilot, and the extra returns are mostly low-acuity |
| Case mix or seasonal change unrelated to the AI | Units or periods without the AI show a similar rise (for example, the same months last year, or a comparable ER) |
| Planned follow-ups or a change in how returns are counted | The metric definition changed, or many of the returns were scheduled |

### What to do now
1. **Chart review of the return visits right away**: acuity on return, admissions, serious outcomes, and whether the original AI triage level matched clinician judgment. This is the cheapest check that could change the decision.
2. **If continuing at small scale**: a clinician (triage nurse or physician) makes the final triage call, can override the AI, and assesses vitals and presentation independently rather than just confirming the AI's output. Track override rates and disagreements. Human review reduces risk but doesn't remove it, because automation bias is real.
3. **Set a stopping rule in advance**, for example a defined rise in high-acuity returns or serious adverse events that triggers an immediate pause.
4. **Get a better comparison**: a concurrent comparison, ideally randomized or balanced (by shift, day or site), with and without the AI, measuring waits, returns, return severity and left-without-being-seen rates together. Even that won't catch very rare harms without enough volume and follow-up time.

### Where this could be wrong
The strongest counterargument is that long ER waits harm patients too, so a pause gives up a real benefit, and the rise in returns may turn out to be harmless (for example, more patients now being seen). If the chart review shows that, expanding the system with continued monitoring becomes reasonable. If it shows missed serious conditions among patients the AI triaged, pause.
