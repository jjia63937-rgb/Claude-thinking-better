**Recommendation: don't roll it out. Keep testing on a smaller scale, and treat the rise in return visits as a safety signal that must be explained before any expansion. If the hospital can't monitor it closely, pause it.**

**Why not a full rollout**
- A 15% rise in 30-day return visits could mean patient harm. The system may be sending people home too quickly, under-triaging them, or missing diagnoses. In an ER, a missed serious condition costs far more than shorter waits save.
- The two numbers may be linked. Waits could be shorter partly *because* patients are being moved through too fast, and those same patients then come back. If so, the 20% gain is partly an illusion: the work has just been moved into a second visit.
- With no study design, we can't tell whether the AI caused either change. Seasonality, staffing changes, a different patient mix, a change in how return visits were counted, or ordinary random variation could explain one or both. Rolling out a system on uncontrolled evidence that includes a possible harm signal is hard to defend.

**Why not simply pause**
- The wait-time improvement is large and valuable. Crowded ERs cause harm of their own through delayed care and patients leaving before being seen.
- The rise in returns may not be harmful. Relative percentages can hide small absolute numbers: 15% of a small base could be a few patients. Some returns are planned follow-ups or unrelated visits. And if patients who used to leave without being seen are now treated and asked to come back, return visits would rise for a good reason.
- Stopping now throws away the chance to learn which explanation is true.

**Conditions for continuing on a smaller scale**
1. **Safety first:** clinicians keep final authority over triage decisions, and there is a clear way to override the AI.
2. **Look into the return visits right away:** review the charts of patients who came back. Were the returns related to the first visit? Were they more serious, such as admissions, ICU stays or deaths? Did the AI under-triage these patients? Split the results by acuity level and by patient group to check for bias.
3. **Report absolute numbers** for both results, not just percentage changes.
4. **Use a better design:** a randomized or stepped-wedge design, or at least a concurrent control group (AI vs. standard triage over the same period), so the effect can be separated from outside trends.
5. **Set stopping rules in advance:** for example, pause at once if return visits that lead to admission, or serious adverse events, go above an agreed threshold.
6. **Set rollout criteria in advance:** expand only if the wait-time benefit holds up and harmful return visits are no higher than under standard triage.

**Bottom line:** the evidence is too thin to justify a full rollout, and too promising to simply abandon. Continue on a smaller scale under close monitoring, with a proper control group, and make the return-visit rise the main question to answer. If early chart review shows under-triage is causing harmful returns, pause the system.
