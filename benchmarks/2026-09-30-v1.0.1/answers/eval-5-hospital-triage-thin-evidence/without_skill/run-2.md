**My recommendation: don't roll it out hospital-wide yet. Keep testing it on a smaller scale, and add safety guardrails and a much better evaluation design. If a quick look shows the extra return visits involve patients who were harmed, pause it.**

## Why not roll out
The 15% rise in 30-day return visits is a possible safety signal. In emergency care, a patient who comes back within 30 days may have been under-triaged, sent home too early or misdiagnosed. A shorter wait is worth having, but it doesn't make up for patients who were discharged when they shouldn't have been. Scaling up now would spread an unexplained risk across the whole hospital.

## Why not stop it outright
Two numbers without any study design can't show that the AI caused either change. Other explanations fit the data just as well:
- **Relative vs. absolute numbers.** If the return rate went from 4% to 4.6%, that's a small rise. If it went from 20% to 23%, that's a serious one. We don't know which.
- **Confounders.** Season (for example, flu), changes in patient mix, staffing changes or other process changes during the pilot could explain either number.
- **Faster flow may shift care rather than worsen it.** Quicker discharges plus planned follow-up visits could raise return visits without anyone being harmed.
- **Chance.** A small pilot can produce swings this size from noise alone.
- **Mechanism.** Some of the drop in wait time may come from the triage workflow and not from the AI's judgment.

A 20% cut in waits is also a real benefit, since long waits hurt patients too. Stopping outright would throw away a possible gain before anyone knows whether the harm is real.

## What "continue testing" should mean in practice
1. **Review the return visits right away.** Pull the charts and ask: were the returns unplanned? Were they for the same complaint? Did they lead to admissions, ICU stays or deaths? Were the patients ones the AI assigned low acuity? Have clinicians review a sample for missed diagnoses.
2. **Set a stop rule in advance.** If the review finds more serious unplanned returns or clear under-triage, pause the system at once.
3. **Add guardrails while testing continues.** Keep clinicians in the loop and let them override the AI. Double-check patients the AI rates as low acuity who belong to high-risk groups (older patients, chest pain, and so on).
4. **Improve the study design.** Use a concurrent control group, such as randomizing by shift or day, or a stepped-wedge rollout. Measure absolute rates with confidence intervals, and track outcomes by subgroup and acuity level. Balancing measures should include return visits that lead to admission, mortality and the number of patients who leave without being seen.
5. **Agree on success criteria ahead of time.** One example: the system scales only if waits fall and serious unplanned 30-day returns are no higher than the control group's, within a pre-set margin.

## Bottom line
The data shows a possible efficiency gain alongside a possible safety cost, and it can't prove either one. When a signal that patients may be harmed is present and still unexplained, the burden of proof falls on the rollout. Keep testing at a limited scale, look into the return visits now, and be ready to pause.
