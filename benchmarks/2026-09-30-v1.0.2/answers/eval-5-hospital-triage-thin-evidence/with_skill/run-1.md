**Recommendation: don't roll it out. Keep testing on a smaller, closely watched scale, but only while a quick review of the extra return visits happens. If that review shows patients were harmed or sent home too early, pause the system.** This depends on something we don't know yet: whether the extra returns are clinically serious and caused by the AI's triage decisions. Two numbers can't answer that.

**Why not roll out now**
A shorter wait is a real benefit, since long waits cause their own harm. But the rise in return visits is the kind of signal that can mean patients were under-triaged or discharged too soon. In triage, a missed serious case costs far more than extra waiting time. Rolling out hospital-wide would spread an unexplained safety signal before anyone understands it.

**Why not simply pause**
The rise in returns might not be harm at all, and pausing gives up the shorter waits. A limited, supervised test keeps collecting the evidence you need to decide.

**What the two numbers can't tell you (all unknown, not assumed)**
- Whether "15%" is a relative increase (for example, from 10% to 11.5%) or 15 percentage points. The second would be alarming and would argue for an immediate pause.
- How the comparison was made: before and after, or against a concurrent control group. We also don't know how many patients were involved or how long the pilot ran.
- What the returns were: the same complaint, admitted on return, serious outcomes, or planned or minor follow-ups.

**Competing explanations, and what would tell them apart**
- **The AI under-triages some patients** → returning patients were given lower acuity by the AI than their condition warranted, and the returns involve the same complaint or end in admission.
- **Faster throughput, not the AI's scoring, drives earlier discharges** → the returns trace back to shorter treatment or observation time, not to the triage level assigned.
- **Case mix or season changed** (flu season, a different patient population) → the rise shows up across patients whatever triage level the AI gave them, and in comparable periods or sites without the AI.
- **Measurement changed** (how "return visit" or "wait" was defined or recorded) → check whether the definitions and data sources were the same before and during the pilot.

**Cheapest check that could change the decision:** a clinician chart review of the pilot's 30-day return visits. Look at whether each was the same complaint, the severity on return, admissions and adverse events, and whether the AI's triage level differed from what a clinician would have assigned. Clear harm linked to the AI means pause. Benign or unrelated returns mean continue.

**How to run the smaller test**
- Compare against a concurrent control group, with the AI assigned by randomized or alternating shifts or days, instead of before and after. This separates the AI's effect from seasonal and case-mix changes. With small numbers, a short trial can still miss rare harms, so "no harm found" won't mean "shown safe."
- Set stopping rules in advance for return visits, admissions on return, and adverse events.
- The triage nurse or physician makes the final triage decision and can override the AI. They need to see the vital signs and complaint independently, not just the AI's score, or human review becomes a rubber stamp. Track how often they override, and give extra scrutiny to cases where the AI assigns a lower acuity.

**Strongest counterargument:** if the returns turn out to be minor, then holding back loses a 20% cut in waits, which also protects patients. That is why the recommendation is a fast, targeted review rather than an open-ended pause.

**Confidence:** moderate that a limited, monitored test beats either extreme. The answer would change mainly on what the review of the return visits finds.
