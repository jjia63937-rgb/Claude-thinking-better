**Recommendation: don't roll it out. Keep testing on a smaller scale under tighter controls. But first do a quick review of the extra return visits, and pause the system if they turn out to involve real harm.** The 15% rise in return visits is a possible safety signal. It could also be harmless. Nothing we have tells those two cases apart, and the decision depends on which one it is.

**Why not a full rollout.** The pilot gives two results, and they point in opposite directions. Faster triage is a real benefit. But one explanation fits both numbers at once: the system may be moving patients through faster by under-triaging some of them, and those patients then come back. If that is what's happening, a 20% cut in waiting time is being paid for with missed or delayed care. With no information on how the pilot was designed, we also can't tell whether the AI caused either change.

**Why not an automatic pause.** Shorter ER waits also protect patients: fewer people leave without being seen, and time-sensitive conditions are treated sooner. Switching the system off gives that up. So it isn't clearly the safe choice, unless the extra return visits are harmful.

**What the return-visit rise could mean.** Each explanation comes with the check that would confirm or rule it out:
- **The AI under-triages some patients**, so they get discharged too early. *Check:* at the return visit, were these patients admitted, sicker, or given a diagnosis that was missed the first time? Were they disproportionately patients the AI rated low-acuity?
- **Faster flow puts discharge pressure on clinicians**, separate from the AI's triage accuracy. *Check:* compare the AI's triage level with what a clinician would have assigned for the patients who returned.
- **The patient mix or the season changed** during the pilot. *Check:* compare against the same period last year, or against sites that didn't use the AI.
- **The returns are harmless**: scheduled follow-ups, or more low-acuity patients using the ER now that waits are shorter. *Check:* classify the returns as planned or unplanned, and look at their severity.
- **Measurement.** "15%" might be a relative increase or percentage points, and "return visit" may have been defined differently than before. *Check:* confirm the definition and the baseline.

**How to continue the test:**
1. **Right away:** clinicians review the charts of the return visits. This is the check most likely to change the decision. If it finds missed diagnoses or serious deterioration, pause the system until that is understood.
2. **Narrow the scope:** use the system only where under-triage costs the least, or run it as advice alongside standard triage rather than in its place.
3. **Keep a clinician as the final decision-maker.** The nurse or physician must be able to override the AI and should assess the patient independently (vital signs, their own judgment) instead of just approving the AI's score. Human review lowers the risk but doesn't remove it: people tend to go along with an automated rating. So also track how often clinicians override the AI, and why.
4. **Use a better comparison:** randomize or alternate AI and standard triage by shift or by day, so both groups are measured at the same time. That separates the AI's effect from seasonal and staffing changes. It still won't catch rare harms unless the test is large enough, so set stopping rules in advance.
5. **Measure the outcomes that matter:** unplanned returns within 72 hours and 30 days, admissions at the return visit, ICU transfers, deaths, patients who left without being seen, and under-triage rate against a clinician reference, broken down by age, language and other high-risk groups.

**Confidence:** fairly high that a full rollout is premature on this evidence. Whether to keep testing or pause depends mainly on what the return visits turn out to be. If a chart review shows they are mostly planned or minor, the case for continuing is strong. If it shows missed serious conditions, pause.
