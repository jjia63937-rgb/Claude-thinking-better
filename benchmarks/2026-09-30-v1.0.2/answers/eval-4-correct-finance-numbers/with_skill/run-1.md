**Yes, the numbers are right, assuming all 1,200 promo customers would otherwise pay the full $50. I'd tighten two phrases before you send it.**

**The math**
- Price after the January increase: $40 × 1.25 = **$50**
- Promo price: $50 × 0.80 = **$40**. That is exactly last year's price, because 1.25 × 0.80 = 1.00.
- Promo cost against the post-increase price: 1,200 × ($50 − $40) = **$12,000/month**

**Wording to tighten**
1. **"the same $40 they paid last year"** is only literally true for customers who were subscribed last year. Anyone who joined in 2026 never paid $40 before. Safer: "the promo price ($40) equals last year's list price."
2. **"the promo costs us $12,000/month"** is the price we're giving up, not necessarily lost revenue. It assumes all 1,200 would have stayed on at $50 without the promo. If some would have churned or never signed up, the real cost is lower, and the promo could even pay for itself. A finance lead will probably pick up on this, so call it "$12,000/month in discounts vs. the $50 list price."

Also, "revenue per promo customer is unchanged" compares against last year. Against the plan you raised prices to reach, it's $10 lower per customer. That's the same point as the $12k figure, so make sure the email doesn't read as if it says both "no impact" and "costs $12k" without explaining which baseline each one uses.

Suggested line: *"Promo customers pay $40/month, the same as the pre-increase list price. Against the new $50 price, the promo means $12,000/month in discounts across 1,200 customers (before any retention or acquisition effect)."*
